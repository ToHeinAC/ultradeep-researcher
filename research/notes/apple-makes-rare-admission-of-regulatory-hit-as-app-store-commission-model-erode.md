# Apple Makes Rare Admission of Regulatory Hit as App Store Commission Model Erodes, Weighing on $100 Billion Services Business
Apple has for the first time acknowledged in a regulatory filing that global antitrust actions are eroding its services business, which generates more than $100 billion in annual revenue. The company warned that transactions completed through alternative payment systems "may be entirely ineligible for commission." Sensor Tower data shows US App Store consumer spending fell 6% year-over-year in the second quarter, versus 9% growth a year earlier; Appfigures estimates Apple's US commission revenue has shrunk 18% so far this year. Apple's services revenue for the third quarter of fiscal 2026 hit a record $30.7 billion but missed Wall Street expectations. CFO Kevan Parekh acknowledged that rule changes have already impacted the segment. Analysts note that Apple's premium valuation rests on its services business, and the market will be forced to reassess that segment's profitability.

Apple has for the first time explicitly acknowledged in its latest regulatory filing that intensifying antitrust regulatory pressure worldwide is materially eroding the core profit foundation of its services business. The company warned that purchases completed through alternative payment systems "may be entirely ineligible for commission" — a statement widely viewed as the clearest signal yet that the App Store commission model underpinning its high-margin services business is facing systemic loosening.
Earlier this month, Apple reported services revenue and margins that both fell short of Wall Street expectations. While services revenue for the third quarter of fiscal 2026 reached a record $30.7 billion, growth momentum has visibly weakened. Apple CFO Kevan Parekh acknowledged during the earnings discussion that recent App Store rule changes have already had a tangible impact on the segment.
### Cracks in the Commission Model Under Regulatory Siege
Apple's grip on the App Store is being challenged simultaneously across multiple jurisdictions. Last year, game developer Epic Games accused Apple of monopolistic practices in violation of federal antitrust law, ultimately forcing Apple to allow app developers to direct users to alternative payment methods outside the App Store free of charge — bypassing its traditional commission mechanism.
The shockwaves from that ruling quickly reached consumers. According to mobile app analytics firm Sensor Tower, US consumer spending through the App Store fell 6% year-over-year in the second quarter, compared with 9% growth in the same period last year. Sensor Tower explicitly noted that the US slowdown reflects a "significant impact" on App Store consumer spending from the ruling.
Globally, consumer spending through the App Store grew just 3% year-over-year in the first half of this year, a sharp deceleration from 13% growth a year earlier. Sensor Tower also noted that weak consumer spending and macroeconomic uncertainty may have dampened demand to some degree.
Estimates from Appfigures, another app data research firm, are even more sobering: Apple's US commission revenue has contracted 18% so far this year. Appfigures research also found that App Store revenue in Brazil and Japan is contracting as well, following new regulatory measures introduced in those countries in recent months.

### Analyst Divide: Currency Effects or Structural Shift?
Wall Street analysts are split on how to interpret the services slowdown. UBS analyst David Vogt bluntly characterized the App Store growth deceleration as a "concern," suggesting it may not be a short-term fluctuation. Bank of America analyst Wamsi Mohan acknowledged that the $30.7 billion services figure set a record, but conceded it was "slightly below our expectations."
Apple management has attempted to attribute some of the earnings pressure to external factors such as currency fluctuations, but Parekh's candid admission of regulatory impact makes that explanation appear insufficient. Nicholas Rodelli, director of legal research at Washington Analysis, offered a sharper assessment: "Apple's premium valuation rests on its services business, and the App Store is at the heart of that business. We believe the market will reassess the profitability of the services segment."
Rodelli's judgment touches on a deeper issue: Apple's valuation logic has long relied on the high margins of its services business to offset cyclicality in its hardware segment. If App Store commission revenue remains under sustained pressure, investors may need to recalibrate fundamental assumptions about Apple's long-term profitability.
### Multi-Front Global Regulatory Assault
The regulatory pressure Apple faces is not an isolated event but part of a broader global antitrust wave. In the United States, the precedent set by the Epic Games case is being invoked by a growing number of developers; in Europe, the Digital Markets Act imposes strict obligations on large tech platforms, forcing Apple to open up sideloading and third-party payments; in Asia, regulators in Japan and Brazil have followed suit with new rules that further compress Apple's commission revenue.
Notably, Apple's language in the regulatory filing — "may be entirely ineligible for commission" — hints at a worst-case scenario of revenue falling to zero. While the phrasing carries the legal caution typical of risk disclosures, its appearance in Apple's official filing is itself a landmark event. Previously, Apple had consistently maintained that its commission model was a fair and reasonable industry standard, and had kept an optimistic tone about the potential impact of regulatory challenges.
The services business has long been the pillar of Apple's growth strategy. With hardware products like the iPhone facing slowing growth, Apple CEO Tim Cook has repeatedly emphasized services as the core engine of the company's transformation. Now, the fuel powering that engine is being progressively diminished by regulatory forces. Whether Apple can find alternative growth pathways will be a key focus for the market in the coming quarters.
