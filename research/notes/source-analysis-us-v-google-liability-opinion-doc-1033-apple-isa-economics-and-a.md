## Thesis / Central claim

Judge Mehta holds that Google is a monopolist in two markets (general search services; general search text ads) and unlawfully maintained that monopoly under Sherman Act §2 through its web of exclusive distribution agreements — chief among them the Apple Internet Services Agreement (ISA). The court finds the ISA's default-exclusivity, not the revenue-share payment mechanism itself, to be the unlawful conduct; the payments matter legally only insofar as they are the *consideration* that makes the exclusivity durable and disincentivizes Apple from entering search itself. Separately and independently, the court finds Google exercises its search-market monopoly power by charging supracompetitive prices for text ads — a second and distinct vulnerability in the revenue pool from which Apple's TAC payment is drawn.

## Methodology / Basis of claims

Findings of fact rest on a nine-week bench trial (Sept.–Nov. 2023), sworn testimony from named executives on both sides (Eddy Cue and John Giannandrea for Apple; Sundar Pichai, Prabhakar Raghavan, Elizabeth Reid for Google; Satya Nadella and Mikhail Parakhin for Microsoft; Gabriel Weinberg for DuckDuckGo), over 3,500 admitted exhibits including the actual contracts (JX1, JX2, JX4–JX33, JX97 — the ISA and its amendments from 2002 through the 2021 extension), internal company documents/analyses (e.g., Google's "Alice in Wonderland" study, Apple's internal Bing-switch financial model UPX273), and quantitative discovery productions (Apple/Google query-volume and revenue-share data, Mozilla's own default-switch experiments). The court applies the *Microsoft* (D.C. Cir. 2001) exclusive-dealing framework throughout — this is the load-bearing legal test, not a market-share bright-line rule. Numeric findings are numbered as "FOF ¶ [n]" (findings of fact) and cross-referenced in the legal analysis; several exact current-period figures (notably the current % revenue-share rate Google pays Apple) are REDACTED in the public version of the opinion, so the analysis below flags where an exact number is unavailable in this source and only a historical anchor or an aggregate ($ or %) is disclosed.

## Key findings / Claims (with specific numbers where present)

### 1. The money — ISA payment history and structure

1. **2022 payment: ~$20 billion, worldwide.** "In 2022, Google's revenue share payment to Apple was an estimated $20 billion (worldwide queries)." Sourced to Cue's testimony (Tr. at 2492:22–2493:6), reported as the court's own finding at FOF ¶299 (body ¶299, opinion p.103). VERIFIED.
2. **2020 payment: ~17.5% of Apple's operating profit.** "This is nearly double the payment made in 2020, which was then equivalent to 17.5% of Apple's operating profit." (Cue, Tr. at 2492:2-8; Whinston discussing UPXD104 at 19). This implies the 2020 dollar figure was roughly half of $20B, i.e., on the order of $10B (the opinion does not spell out the exact 2020 dollar amount, only the ~2x growth ratio and the profit-share percentage — both testimony-sourced, not the court's independent calculation). VERIFIED, with the caveat that the precise 2020 dollar figure is inferred from the "nearly double" framing, not stated outright.
3. **2022 payment exceeds all Google's other revenue-share payments to all partners COMBINED, and is ~2x that combined total.** FOF ¶299 (Whinston, Tr. at 5727:5-20, discussing UPXD104 at 19). VERIFIED.
4. **Current revenue-share percentage is REDACTED** in the public opinion text everywhere it appears (FOF ¶298, opinion p.102-103; also in the exclusivity analysis at p.208: "Google pays Apple % of its net ad revenue, which amounted to $20 billion in 2022"). The court never discloses the exact current rate in unredacted text. The best disclosed historical anchor is **33.75%**, described as "Google's then-33.75% revenue share" at the time of the 2016 ISA negotiation (FOF ¶328, opinion p.114, sourced to Roszak testimony and UPX674 at 914). The current rate (post-2016/2021) is not disclosed in this document — any current-rate figure used downstream (e.g., commonly cited ~36%) is NOT sourced to this opinion and must be flagged as external estimate.
5. **Contract term/renewal history.** Original ISA: 2002 (non-exclusive, no revenue share). 2005 amendment: first revenue share, $10M one-time + 50% of ad revenue, 3-year term. 2007: extended to iPhone/mobile. 2009 and 2012: Apple twice sought non-exclusive/flexible terms and was refused by Google, which conditioned any revenue share on maintaining exclusivity (FOF ¶¶319-320). 2014 "Joint Cooperation Agreement": Google remained exclusive default; added "default bookmarks" mechanic. **Current ISA signed 2016** (JX33), **extended 2021 for five years to 2026** (JX97 at 357), with an Apple-unilateral option to extend two more years to 2028, and mutual-agreement extension possible to 2031. **Neither party can unilaterally terminate before the 2026 (or extended) termination date** — the 2016 renegotiation specifically removed the prior at-will termination right (JX33 at 800). The ISA also obligates both parties to *cooperate to defend the agreement*, including against regulatory action (opinion p.102, FOF ¶292). This is directly relevant to the "what remedy can a court compel and on what timetable" question: the contract itself is legally locked through at least 2026, and the parties are contractually bound to jointly resist the very kind of regulatory action this case represents.
6. **Query volume mechanics underlying the payment.** ~10 billion user queries per week across Siri/Spotlight/Safari; ~80% via Safari. 65% of all Apple-device queries flow through the Safari *default* access point; 61.8% on mobile specifically; only 5.1% of all iPhone searches use a non-Google GSE, meaning **Google captures ~95% of all general search queries on iPhones** (FOF ¶296, opinion p.102-103, Tr. 9758:9–9759:22 discussing DXD37 at 52). Revenue share is paid on Safari AND Chrome queries (even though Apple doesn't preload Chrome) (FOF ¶298).
7. **Google's own internal loss estimates if it lost the Apple default**: 2016 estimate — Google would recover only 30% of query volume on iOS but 70% on macOS if it lost the default (UPX142 at 886); 2020 estimate — projected 60–80% query loss on iOS (UPX148 at 826). Google separately projected it would lose ~65% of its Apple-derived revenue overall without the ISA, even assuming it retained some users (UPX1050 at 886, FOF ¶300).

### 2. The alternative-bidder floor — the decisive economic finding for the query

8. **Microsoft/Bing's 2015-16 offers, and why Apple declined.** Microsoft offered Apple a 90% revenue share (~$20B over five years) — rejected. Microsoft then escalated to **offering 100%+ of Bing's revenue, or even offering to sell Bing to Apple outright** — still rejected (FOF ¶323, Tr. at 2511:14-14, 2530:14-21 (Cue)).
9. **Google's "Alice in Wonderland" internal study** (named for Microsoft/"Alice") calculated Microsoft would need to pay Apple **122% of Bing's revenue share just to match Google's then-33.75% revenue share** — and concluded "it will not be possible for Alice to match our payments profitably" (FOF ¶328, UPX674 at 914, Roszak Tr. at 1683:10-13). The court explicitly states this internal Google prediction "turned out to be wholly accurate": Microsoft did in fact offer 100%+ revenue share plus guarantees, and Apple still declined (opinion p.251).
10. **Apple's own internal financial modeling (Cue, 2016, UPX273) quantified the gap starkly**: staying with Google would be worth ~$40B over the next five years and ~$70B over the following five (total ~$110B/10yr) — versus Microsoft's ~$20B five-year offer. Cue internally concluded a Microsoft deal "doesn't match Google ($30B v. $40B)" even under an aggressive counter-proposal (Apple asking Microsoft to guarantee $4B year one, stepping up $1B/year to $30B cumulative) and that switching would only make sense if Apple "view[ed] Google as somebody [they] don't want to be in business with" — otherwise "it [was] a no brainer to stay with Google as it is as close to a sure thing as can be" (UPX273 at 974; Tr. 2528:13-16).
11. **The court's and Apple's explicit finding on WHY**: not price, but quality and monetization capability. Cue testified: "If you have an inferior search engine, customers wouldn't use it, and so, therefore, I don't know how you could monetize it well" (Tr. 2510:25–2511:11). And, decisively: "there was 'no price that Microsoft could ever offer [Apple]' to make the switch" (Tr. 2519:10-11); "I don't believe there's a price in the world that Microsoft could offer us. They offered to give us Bing for free. They could give us the whole company." (Tr. 2530:17-19).
12. **Google itself understood Bing posed no real threat during negotiations**, "which minimized Apple's leverage" (opinion p.114, Tr. 7772:12–7773:10, Pichai) — i.e., the court finds Google knowingly underpaid Apple relative to what a competitive market would require, BECAUSE Google knew Apple had no comparable alternative. Pichai testified Google took "into account" that Apple had no other viable option, "which was why [it] didn't pay the [higher] share Apple wanted" (Tr. 7772:12–7773:10).
13. **DuckDuckGo was never a real contender** either: Apple's senior leadership (Cue, Giannandrea) never seriously considered DDG for the default, in part because DDG syndicates from Bing ("a veneer on top of other search engines") and its search quality is viewed as inferior — "it's not a great search engine ... it's not good enough" (Cue, Tr. 2506:12-16).
14. **Apple's own 2021 "Aethon" quality study** found Google superior to Bing on relevance across nearly all access points except desktop, with a "much larger lead on Mobile than Desktop" and particular strength on long-tail queries (UPX260 at 681, FOF ¶333).
15. **Court's ultimate legal conclusion on this point**: "Google's distribution agreements thus appear reasonably capable of having significantly contributed to disincentivizing Microsoft from enlarging its investment in search" (opinion p.243) and, separately, Microsoft's Nadella testified that if Bing hypothetically exited the market entirely, "there would be a real concern as to whether Google would even pay Apple for default status, given the lack of any other option at all" (Tr. 3505:12-17). This is the clearest textual support for the claim that the $20B reflects Google's unique/monopoly position, not a competitively-discovered market price for the Apple default.

### 3. Apple's own incentives and behaviour — court's characterization

16. Apple is portrayed throughout as a **sophisticated, self-interested, repeat negotiator**, not a coerced party. It repeatedly (2009, 2012) sought more flexible/non-exclusive terms and was refused by Google, which conditioned revenue share on exclusivity (FOF ¶¶319-320) — this shows Apple *wanted* more optionality but lacked leverage to get it, not that it was forced into the deal against its commercial interest.
17. Apple has invested materially in search infrastructure since 2018 (hiring ex-Google-Search head John Giannandrea; building crawling/indexing/knowledge-graph capability; unspecified but described as "quite a lot" of dollars and headcount — figures redacted in FOF ¶301) but has **deliberately chosen not to launch a general search engine**, per Giannandrea's testimony (Tr. 2247:17-21).
18. Apple's own internal 2018 assessment: even assuming it retained 80% of queries after a hypothetical split from Google, Apple projected it would **lose over $12 billion in revenue during the first five years** post-separation (UPX460 at 176-77).
19. Court's own reasoning on why Apple stays out: "The prospect of losing tens of billions in guaranteed revenue from Google — which presently come at little to no cost to Apple — disincentivizes Apple from launching its own search engine when it otherwise has built the capacity to do so." (opinion p.242). Crucially, the court explicitly states **the payments need not be Apple's sole reason for staying out** to constitute an anticompetitive effect — a legal standard, not a claim that Apple is unwillingly captured.
20. Apple gets real product upside from the relationship too: Pichai testified "Apple benefits and sells more iPhones by having their brand associated with the quality ... of Google Search" (Tr. 7780:21-23, FOF ¶133) — i.e., the court records Google's view that the relationship is mutually beneficial, not extractive of Apple.
21. Apple retains — and exercises — some independence within the ISA: Suggestions (diverts navigational queries away from Google entirely) and expanded Siri query-routing are contractually unconstrained (per Cue and Giannandrea's own testimony, FOF ¶307), and Apple has "no intentions or plans" to monetize Spotlight with ads regardless of the ISA's right-of-first-refusal clause (Cue, Tr. 2497:11-25).
22. **Bottom line on characterization**: the court frames Apple as a **willing, well-compensated, and economically rational counterparty** that negotiated hard, lost some negotiating rounds, but ultimately stays in the arrangement because it is "close to a sure thing" — not because it is coerced. This has a direct implication for how Apple would react to a remedy: Apple has consistently sought optionality (not exit), and its own testimony and internal documents show it treats the Google payment as a call option value it does not want to give up voluntarily, but would adapt to a changed environment (e.g., its GenAI moves — OpenAI/ChatGPT integration referenced in the companion remedies opinion) rather than fight to preserve the specific exclusivity structure per se.

### 4. Legal theory and remedy-scope constraints

23. **What was found unlawful**: Google's "distribution agreements are exclusive and have anticompetitive effects" and Google "has not offered valid procompetitive justifications" for them (opinion p.3-4, the court's own four-point summary). The court applies *Microsoft*'s de facto/partial exclusivity doctrine (ZF Meritor; LePage's) — the ISA need not contain an express exclusivity clause to be treated as exclusive; "its terms in combination with Apple's established business practices" (Apple's blanket refusal to preload any third-party app) make Google "the only GSE preloaded on an Apple device," which "makes it exclusive" (opinion p.209-210).
24. **What was NOT found unlawful, as to Apple specifically** — this bounds remedy scope tightly: (a) the "Substantially Similar" clause (constraining Apple's Suggestions expansion) — court found **no actual competitive harm shown**, since Apple testified it doesn't view the clause as limiting it at all (opinion p.242-243); (b) the Spotlight "right of first refusal" ad clause — court found the evidence "thin," since Apple doesn't presently monetize Spotlight (opinion p.243-244); (c) Branch (deep-linking, not a GSE) — court found the "nascent-threat" evidence "far weaker than in Microsoft," though it did find some "chilling effect" on distribution partners (opinion p.244-246, re: Samsung/AT&T, not Apple specifically).
25. **The revenue-share payment mechanism itself is NOT the unlawful conduct** — the exclusivity condition attached to it is. The court is explicit that a firm "may itself benefit from" an exclusive agreement without that benefit rendering it non-exclusive (opinion p.209-210) and separately that Google's use of revenue-share size as a lever to secure exclusivity — not the existence of revenue share per se — drives the anticompetitive effect finding. This is the single most important legal-theory point for remedy design: **a remedy that ends exclusivity while permitting Google to continue paying Apple for a non-exclusive default (or non-default) placement would be consistent with the liability finding**; a remedy that banned payment outright would go beyond what this liability finding requires. (The original 2002-2007 pre-2005/pre-revenue-share ISA, and the fact that "the absence of exclusivity did not stunt Apple's product development" historically, opinion p.253, is cited by the court itself as evidence that a non-exclusive default-with-payment arrangement is a viable, less-restrictive counterfactual — this is squarely on point for remedy design and should be flagged to the depth investigator on RQ2.)
26. **Separate, independent theory of liability on ad pricing**: Google's exercise of monopoly power via supracompetitive text-ad pricing (88% share of general search text ads market in 2020, up from 80% in 2016; 20%+ y/y ad revenue growth; price increases made "without regard" to competitors' prices, FOF ¶¶192, 232-233, 259, 263-267) is found to be **monopolistic conduct independent of the distribution-agreement exclusivity theory**. This matters for durability: even a remedy that leaves the ISA/TAC structure untouched could shrink the underlying ad-revenue pool Apple's percentage is drawn from, if a future case or the ongoing ad-tech litigation curtails Google's pricing power. (Cross-reference, not a finding of this opinion: the opinion cites *Epic Games v. Apple*, 67 F.4th 984, by analogy for the same "supracompetitive commission ... without regard to anything other than legal action" logic applied to Apple's own App Store commissions — this is Apple's OWN Section-1 exposure on the commission side, useful for the paper's broader durability taxonomy but not a finding against Apple in this case.)
27. Liability was found only for Counts I and III (general search services market; general search text ads market) of both the DOJ and Colorado complaints; Google was **found NOT to have monopoly power in the broader "search advertising" market**, and the SA360 (search-ads-platform interoperability) claim against Google was rejected entirely under the "no duty to deal" doctrine (Trinko/Aspen Skiing framework) — narrowing the scope of what any remedy can touch.
28. Remedies were explicitly **bifurcated** from liability by joint party request (opinion p.5-6) — this document contains NO remedy design; that is exclusively in the companion Doc. 1436 (Sept. 2, 2025) note. Do not draw remedy-design content from this note.

### 5. Generative AI / successor search interfaces

29. The liability opinion (evidentiary record built through the 2023 trial) already engages with GenAI, treating it as real but not yet transformative competition: Google launched Bard "in direct response" to Microsoft's BingChat/Copilot announcement — "a clear example of Google responding to competition" (FOF ¶¶111-112, opinion p.247).
30. **Explicit court finding, quotable verbatim**: "AI technologies have the potential to transform search" but, as of the 2023-24 record, **"AI has not supplanted the traditional ingredients that define general search"** and "it is not likely to do so anytime soon" (FOF ¶114, citing Raghavan's testimony that this is "a journey... I think there's a few more steps to go"). Further: "generative AI has not (or at least, not yet) eliminated or materially reduced the need for user data to deliver quality search results" (FOF ¶115).
31. This is a load-bearing 2023-24 evidentiary snapshot, not a live 2025-26 assessment — the fetcher's/orchestrator's other sources should be checked against the companion 2025 remedies opinion (Doc. 1436) for an UPDATED finding, since that later record (per lines observed while disambiguating the scratchpad contamination above) shows GenAI query volume becoming material by 2025 (ChatGPT ~1.2B daily queries vs. Gemini ~140M) and Apple's Safari search volume declining "for the first time in 22 years," which the 2024 liability record could not have captured. **Flag to orchestrator**: the durability argument for TAC should treat the liability opinion's GenAI finding (¶¶111-115) as the 2023-vintage baseline and explicitly note that the subsequent remedies-phase record shows the picture moving, which strengthens rather than weakens the case for treating GenAI substitution risk as a live, time-varying variable rather than a settled non-factor.

### 6. Counter-evidence — findings that cut against "TAC is fragile"

32. **Google's own share among NON-default Apple query flows is very high.** Of the ~35% of Apple queries that flow through non-default access points, and given Google's overall ~95% share of all iPhone queries, simple arithmetic on the court's own figures (FOF ¶296) implies Google captures roughly 85-90% of the queries where users are NOT defaulted to it. This is real evidence that Google's dominance is substantially quality/brand-driven, not purely default-lock-in-driven — supporting Google's own (partially credited, though legally non-dispositive) argument at trial.
33. **Mozilla-Yahoo natural experiment (2014-2017) shows meaningful reversion, but incomplete.** When Firefox's default flipped to Yahoo, Google's share fell from 80-90% to 60-70% (a 20-point drop) — users did NOT fully abandon Google even while it was non-default, and query share partially recovered even before the default reverted, though Google "never [regained] more than 70%" until the default itself was switched back (opinion p.116-117, FOF ¶338). This cuts both ways: it shows real user preference persists absent the default (counter-evidence to full fragility), but also shows the default effect is real and substantial (20 points is not nothing).
34. **Windows/Edge precedent**: even where Bing is the default (Edge browser on Windows), Google's overall Windows share is 80% (via downloaded Chrome), while its share on Edge specifically (where it is not default) is only 20% (FOF ¶¶82-84). This shows sophisticated/high-intent users route around defaults given an accessible alternative pathway (installing Chrome) — evidence that a meaningful fraction of Google's revenue does NOT depend on default placement, at least on platforms where switching cost is low (desktop). The court explicitly credits this as evidence that "defaults are less effective when the alternative has strong brand recognition and product quality" (opinion p.211) — this is Apple-relevant by analogy, since Safari-desktop switching costs are lower than iOS/mobile.
35. **The court itself finds real value in the Google-Apple partnership beyond antitrust harm**: Pichai's uncontested testimony that Apple "benefits and sells more iPhones" from association with Google's search quality (FOF ¶133) — evidence the arrangement is not simply an extraction of Apple by Google but has genuine reciprocal commercial logic that would persist under many counterfactual regulatory regimes.
36. **Choice friction and habituation reduce (but do not eliminate) the "no viable alternative" story's force**: Google's own internal behavioral-economics research documents that many users don't know they have a choice of search engine or how to change it (FOF ¶¶67-69), and that switching costs are much higher on mobile than desktop (FOF ¶¶70-72). This means a chunk of the $20B rents on user *inertia*, not on Google's demonstrated quality edge — which argues the payment is NOT solely "market price for superior quality" but partly a behavioral-lock-in rent that a well-designed remedy (e.g., a choice screen, similar to the EU's Android measure referenced at FOF ¶¶1088-1090) could erode without Apple losing genuine user welfare. This is the strongest counter-evidence to treating the entire $20B as irreplaceable "quality premium" — some meaningful fraction is friction/habit rent that a remedy could plausibly claw back for Apple or redistribute, even if the "best search engine" portion of Google's edge (documented via the Aethon study, ¶333) cannot be replicated by any rival today.

## Caveats, limitations, contradictions

- **Redaction of the current revenue-share percentage** is the single biggest data gap this source cannot fill. Every instance of the current (post-2016) percentage is blacked out in the public opinion. Only the 2016-vintage 33.75% figure survives, embedded in a description of Google's own internal Microsoft-competitiveness model — it is a reasonable historical anchor but should not be presented as the current rate.
- **The 2020 dollar-figure for the ISA payment is not stated explicitly** — only derivable by inference from "nearly double" and 17.5%-of-operating-profit. Any downstream note asserting a precise 2020 dollar figure should show its derivation (Apple's FY2020 operating income × 17.5%) rather than attribute a number to this opinion that the opinion doesn't state.
- **This opinion is entirely pre-remedy.** Every dollar/percentage figure describes the pre-litigation-outcome status quo (through the 2023 trial record); it says nothing about what Apple will actually receive after the Sept. 2025 remedies order takes effect. Any synthesis that uses this note's $20B figure as a forward-looking number without incorporating the remedies opinion's actual disposition would be an error the drafter must avoid.
- **The court's finding on Apple as "willing beneficiary" vs. "disincentivized captive" is nuanced, not clean.** The opinion simultaneously finds (a) Apple actively sought more flexibility and was refused (suggesting some degree of Google leverage over Apple), and (b) Apple would have stayed with Google even under a hypothetical unconstrained choice, given Bing's quality gap (suggesting the arrangement reflects genuine preference, not coercion). Both statements appear in the same document and are not in tension from the court's perspective (the legal test is about effect on THIRD PARTIES' — Microsoft's — ability to compete, not about whether Apple itself was harmed), but a careless reader could quote either in isolation to support opposite framings. The digest above preserves both.
- **GenAI findings are a 2023-24 snapshot** (see item 31) and are already stale relative to the 2025 remedies-phase record and to 2026 market conditions (Apple-OpenAI partnership, Apple's own reported exploration of AI search alternatives per other corpus sources). Treat ¶¶111-115 as a lower bound on how much AI has already eroded the "GSE is uniquely necessary" premise, not as a current assessment.
- No internal contradiction found in the liability findings themselves; the redactions are the main limitation, not any inconsistency.

## Relevance to research_query

This source is directly load-bearing for **both** research questions. For RQ1 (durability classification and quantification), it supplies the only sworn-testimony-sourced multi-year time series on the Apple-Google payment ($20B 2022 worldwide; ~$10B implied 2020; 17.5% of Apple's 2020 operating profit) and, critically, the court's own legal characterization of what specifically is at risk (the *exclusivity*, not the payment mechanism) — which is exactly the evidence needed to justify probability-weighting rather than multiplying TAC in the durability taxonomy. For RQ2 (what remedy can a court compel, at what value), this opinion supplies the alternative-bidder floor analysis that the query's prompt explicitly asks for: the Microsoft/Bing 100%+ offer rejected on quality grounds, Google's own "Alice in Wonderland" confirmation that no competitor can match its payments profitably, and the court's explicit finding that Google exploited Apple's lack of alternatives to underpay relative to a competitive benchmark. This directly substantiates the query's hypothesized analytical implication — no competitor has comparable search quality, so Apple's replacement value for the TAC fee is LOW — and does so with the court's own words, not analyst inference. The source also supplies the crucial remedy-scope constraint (liability attaches to exclusivity, and the pre-2005/pre-revenue-share ISA history shows non-exclusive-default-with-payment is a legally unproblematic counterfactual the court itself points to) which should directly inform how the report models plausible remedy designs and their respective payoffs to Apple. It contributes nothing on the payment-rail/CoreCard or App Store commission legal threads (out of scope for this note; correctly, since the query itself instructs never conflating TAC with payment services).

## Implications for the durability classification

The evidence in this opinion supports classifying Google TAC as **BINARY, not repriceable, and skewed toward the adverse tail** for a 2-5 year holding view — consistent with the query's stated framing, and this source provides the strongest available grounding for that call. The mechanism is precise: liability attaches to *exclusivity*, and the court's own alternative-bidder analysis (Alice in Wonderland; Microsoft's rejected 100%+ offer; Cue's "no price in the world") establishes that Apple's replacement value for the fee, in a world where exclusivity is enjoined, is LOW relative to $20B — because no rival's search quality can monetize comparable volume. That is what makes this "binary" rather than "repriceable": a repriceable asset would see Apple negotiate a modestly lower rate with Google or extract a comparable rate from a substitute; the record here shows neither is available — if a remedy forces a structural change (e.g., an auction, a ban on exclusivity, a choice-screen mandate), the outcome is closer to a step-function (Apple keeps most of the payment under a "default without exclusivity" remedy design that the court itself flags as historically viable, versus Apple losing the bulk of it under a harsher structural remedy) than to a smooth repricing. However, the counter-evidence (section 6 above — Google's very high non-default share on Apple devices, the Windows/Edge precedent, and the meaningful-but-partial Mozilla-Yahoo reversion) means the probability-weighting should NOT be modeled as "either $20B or ~$0" — a plausible middle scenario, well-supported by this record, is a remedy that ends exclusivity but preserves a (still substantial, if somewhat reduced) Google default-and-pay arrangement, given the court's own observation that the pre-2005 non-exclusive-with-preload arrangement worked and that "the absence of exclusivity did not stunt Apple's product development." The report should therefore model at least three discrete states (status quo through 2026-2028 contract term; non-exclusive-but-paid successor arrangement; near-total loss via auction/ban), not a two-point binary, while still treating the distribution as fundamentally discontinuous (binary-like) rather than a continuous repricing curve — this is the nuance the digest recommends the drafter carry forward.

## Extracted quotes

> "Google's 2022 payment under the ISA is more than all of its other revenue share payments combined and is approximately double that combined value."
Court's own finding, FOF ¶299, sourced to Whinston's testimony discussing UPXD104 at 19 — the clearest single data point on Apple's centrality to Google's distribution spend.

> "The analysis concluded that in order for Microsoft to match Google's financial contribution, it would have to pay Apple 122% of Bing's revenue share just to equal Google's then-33.75% revenue share... Google thus determined that 'it will not be possible for Alice to match our payments profitably.'"
FOF ¶328, describing Google's internal "Alice in Wonderland" study — the single most important quantitative finding for the alternative-bidder-floor question in RQ2.

> "I don't believe there's a price in the world that Microsoft could offer us. They offered to give us Bing for free. They could give us the whole company."
Eddy Cue, Tr. at 2530:17-19 — verbatim testimony establishing that no dollar amount, however large, could substitute for Google's search quality in Apple's calculus.

> "The prospect of losing tens of billions in guaranteed revenue from Google—which presently come at little to no cost to Apple—disincentivizes Apple from launching its own search engine when it otherwise has built the capacity to do so. The payments need not be Apple's sole reason for staying out of search to constitute an anticompetitive effect."
Court's own legal conclusion, opinion p.242 — the precise statement of why the payment/exclusivity combination, not the payment alone, is the theory of harm.

> "[E]ven though the ISA contains no express exclusivity provision, its terms in combination with Apple's established business practices means that Google will be the only GSE preloaded on an Apple device. That makes it exclusive."
Opinion p.209-210 — establishes the legal basis for treating the ISA as exclusive despite its facially permissive text, which bounds what a remedy needs to change (the practical exclusivity, achievable via several different contract-text routes) versus what it does not (the payment mechanism as such).

> "AI has not supplanted the traditional ingredients that define general search" and it "is not likely to do so anytime soon."
FOF ¶114 — the court's explicit 2023-24-vintage finding on generative AI's impact on search durability; should be read alongside the later remedies-phase record for an updated picture.

> "Google took 'into account' that Apple had no other viable option 'which was why [it] didn't pay the share Apple wanted.'"
Sundar Pichai, Tr. at 7772:12–7773:10 — Google's own CEO admitting the payment was set below what Apple sought, precisely because Apple lacked a credible alternative — direct evidence the $20B does not reflect a competitively-discovered price.

> "Google's share on Windows devices overall is 80%, but its share on Edge where it is not the default is only 20%."
Opinion p.211, FOF ¶¶82-84 — key counter-evidence data point: users with low switching costs and a strong quality preference route around the default via downloaded Chrome, complicating a pure "no default = no revenue" model.

> "For the foregoing reasons, the court concludes that Google has violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by maintaining its monopoly in two product markets in the United States—general search services and general text advertising—through its exclusive distribution agreements."
Opinion p.276, CONCLUSION — the operative holding that everything else in the opinion supports.
