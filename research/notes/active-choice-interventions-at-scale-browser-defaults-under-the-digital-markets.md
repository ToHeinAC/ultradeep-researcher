  (Kush Amlani and Gemma Petrie) are Mozilla EMPLOYEES, the acknowledgements state
  ''We thank Mozilla for providing data for this research,'' and Mozilla previously
  funded a related project involving several of the same co-authors (Akesson, Chissell,

  — treat accordingly. Additionally, the actual abstract figures DIFFER from what
  Mozilla''s own blog post claimed citing this paper: the paper states Firefox usage
  was 91% higher on iOS and 13% higher on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual
  ''from month 15 onward'' (a point-in-time estimate at/after 15 months), whereas
  Mozilla''s blog claimed ''113% higher... on iOS'' and ''12% higher on Android''
  for ''the 15 months before and after'' rollout — the iOS figure is materially misstated
  (113% vs 91%) and the methodology description differs (a single post-15-month cross-section
  vs. an averaged 15-month window). Substantively, the paper does find genuine market-share
  movement: ''market-share data corroborate movement away from defaults: Safari declined
  on iOS, with gains concentrated in Chrome; Firefox''s smaller gains were meaningful
  relative to baseline. Chrome declined on Android, where it was default, and Firefox
  rose modestly'' — meaning the largest beneficiary of iOS choice screens was Chrome,
  not smaller/privacy browsers, and switching happened away from BOTH dominant defaults
  (Safari and Chrome) toward the other large incumbent as much as toward challengers.
  This complicates the ''lock-in is inertia'' reading: the substitution pattern looks
  more like brand-driven consolidation among incumbents than diffuse consumer empowerment
  toward challenger browsers.'

#  Active Choice Interventions at Scale: Browser Defaults under the Digital Markets Act
[Raul Cepeda Suarez](https://www.nber.org/people/raul2002),

Can active choice mitigate preset-default effects? Using difference-in-differences around the EU Digital Markets Act’s choice-screen mandate, we study Firefox on iOS and Android. From month 15 onward, Firefox usage was 91 percent higher on iOS and 13 percent higher on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual. The platform gap is consistent with rollout differences: iOS reached existing users, while Android primarily reached new devices. Market-share data corroborate movement away from defaults: Safari declined on iOS, with gains concentrated in Chrome; Firefox’s smaller gains were meaningful relative to baseline. Chrome declined on Android, where it was default, and Firefox rose modestly.
