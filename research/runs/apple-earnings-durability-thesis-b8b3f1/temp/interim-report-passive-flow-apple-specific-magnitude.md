# Interim report: passive-flow-apple-specific-magnitude

**Locus question:** What does the demand-system literature imply specifically for how much of APPLE's multiple is passive-flow or ownership-structure artefact, rather than the generic aggregate-market multiplier?
**Flavor:** technical (resumed session — prior investigator completed claim extraction for three papers but was killed before synthesis; this note finishes the job)

## What the corpus already said

Before this session, the corpus held two aggregate-level estimates already reconciled in [[contradiction-graph]] cluster `passive-flow-multiplier-magnitude` (rank 14):

- Gabaix & Koijen (Inelastic Markets Hypothesis): $1 of net flow raises **aggregate** US equity market value by ~$5 (range $3–$8), from an IV estimation showing the implied aggregate demand elasticity is roughly 100x smaller than rational-model benchmarks predict (a 5% price rise cuts demand only ~1%). Source: [[in-search-of-the-origins-of-financial-fluctuations-the-inelastic-markets-hypothe]].
- Bouchaud's Liquidity Theory reinterpretation agrees the multiplier M is "of order unity" (not near zero — markets really are inelastic) but derives it from price-impact/liquidity dynamics, not from inelastic asset demand per se. At a ~monthly horizon (T_m ≈ 20 trading days) it predicts M ≈ 1, well below GK's headline $5. Critically, it predicts **M is proportional to volatility/volume (σ/V)**, so highly-traded, highly-liquid mega-caps should sit toward the LOW end of any cross-sectional multiplier distribution. Source: [[the-inelastic-market-hypothesis]].

The width-sweep's own proposed resolution already anticipated the right shape of the answer ("present passive-flow support as real but likely SMALLER for Apple than the aggregate $5 figure implies... give a range, not a point estimate") but flagged that three passive-flow papers had no extracted claims yet. Those are now extracted (this session) and reported below.

## What the new sources say

**Koijen, Richmond & Yogo, "Which Investors Matter for Equity Valuations and Expected Returns?" (NBER w27402)** — [[which-investors-matter-for-equity-valuations-and-expected-returns-koijen-richmon]]. This is the demand-system paper most likely to speak to individual large-cap stocks, and it is the single most load-bearing new read for this locus. Two findings matter jointly:

1. Its headline counterfactual is aggregate, not firm-level: the 2007.Q4–2016.Q4 active-to-passive shift in institutional wealth produced "an economically significant estimate of **14%**" repricing of the pooled cross-section of the largest 90% of US firms by market equity over 9 years — and of the ~6.1pp decline in aggregate institutional active share over that window (38.9%→32.8%), −4.8pp is attributed to capital *flowing* from active to passive investors (the mechanical/wealth-distribution channel) versus only −1.3pp from investors *changing their strategy* (the behavioral channel) — i.e. the flow-driven component is ~3.7x the behavioral one.
2. I read the full body (not just the extracted claims) to check whether the demand system nonetheless embeds firm-level heterogeneity that could be read off for Apple. It does not, structurally: "Koijen and Yogo (2019) show that β0,i,t < 1 for all investors is a sufficient condition... we impose the coefficient restriction β0,i,t < 1." The own-price elasticity coefficient β0 is estimated **per investor** (or per investor-type, pooled by year) and is held constant across every stock that investor holds — it is not allowed to vary by firm characteristics such as size, liquidity, or trading volume. The model also explicitly restricts to "the largest 90% of firms by market equity" as a single pooled inside-asset universe, with the residual 10% (by count, presumably including illiquid small caps, not large caps) collapsed into one outside asset. There is no term in the estimating equations that would let Apple's own-price elasticity differ from a median large-cap peer's. Zero occurrences of "Apple," "AAPL," "mega-cap," or "large-cap" anywhere in the 23,999-word body. This is a **structural absence**, not an oversight the authors could easily patch: the KRY demand-system architecture is not built to produce single-firm multipliers at all, for Apple or anyone else.

**Easley, Michayluk, O'Hara & Putniņš, "The Active World of Passive Investing"** — [[the-active-world-of-passive-investing-easley-michayluk-ohara-putnins]]. This paper attacks the premise that "passive AUM" is a homogeneous, mechanically price-insensitive pool. Using a constructed Activeness Index (turnover, concentration, flow-performance sensitivity), it finds "most ETFs are active investments in form... or function," and that "Very Active" ETFs alone are 84% of the US equity ETF population by count, rising to 92% with "Moderately Active" included; these active-in-function ETFs hold 58% of aggregate ETF assets. But the AUM-weighted tail matters more for Apple specifically: "Very Passive" ETFs (Activeness Index < 25% — broad cap-weighted index trackers of exactly the kind that must hold Apple near its full index weight) are only 8% of ETFs by count but **42% of aggregate ETF asset value**. So the paper's own logic cuts two ways for this locus: (a) it is evidence against treating headline "passive AUM" totals as a single mechanically inelastic bloc — most of that AUM sits in vehicles with real turnover and selection; but (b) the genuinely mechanical slice is concentrated in a small number of very large vehicles (broad-market cap-weighted funds — Vanguard/BlackRock/State Street total-market and S&P 500 products being the obvious real-world examples), and those are precisely the vehicles for which Apple, as one of the largest constituents of every major US index, has essentially 100% inclusion at close to full index weight. The paper gives no Apple-specific breakdown; this is an inference from its taxonomy, not a stated finding.

**Banafti & Lee, "Inferential Theory for Granular Instrumental Variables in High Dimensions"** — [[inferential-theory-for-granular-instrumental-variables-in-high-dimensions]]. This is a pure econometric-identification extension of the Gabaix-Koijen GIV estimator to large-N, large-T settings; its empirical application is the global crude oil market, not equities, and it contains no equity or Apple-specific estimate. One point is worth carrying forward mechanically: in the GIV framework, market concentration (Herfindahl index of unit sizes) governs the **statistical precision** of the estimator (lower variance when more concentrated), not the **economic magnitude** of the price-impact multiplier. This closes off a tempting but wrong inference — one cannot argue "Apple's ownership register is concentrated among a few giant asset managers, therefore its flow-multiplier is larger" from this paper; concentration affects how confidently GIV-style methods estimate elasticities, not the elasticities themselves. No source in the corpus makes this concentration-implies-magnitude leap explicitly, but it is exactly the kind of intuitive shortcut a report drafting from summaries alone could smuggle in, so it is worth flagging as a dead end.

## Evidence synthesis

The two headline numbers in this locus — GK/KRY's aggregate $3–8 (or 14% over 9 years) and Bouchaud's monthly M≈1 with a σ/V cross-sectional prediction — are not actually in tension about magnitude once the units are separated. They are estimating different things with different assumptions, and neither is stock-specific:

1. **Mechanism disagreement, not just magnitude disagreement.** GK/KRY locate the multiplier in inelastic *asset demand* (investors' willingness to absorb quantity shifts without repricing); Bouchaud locates it in *price impact/liquidity* (order-flow absorption by market-makers, per the square-root law I(Q,T) ≈ Y·σ_T·√(Q/V_T), Y≈0.5 for US stocks, read from the full body of [[the-inelastic-market-hypothesis]]). These give the same order-of-magnitude answer at the aggregate level almost by construction (both are calibrated to match the same observed aggregate inelasticity), but they make opposite-sign predictions about where in the cross-section the multiplier should be largest. GK/KRY's framework has no mechanism that would make Apple different from a median large-cap stock — not because they tested and rejected heterogeneity, but because their estimating equation structurally imposes a constant per-investor elasticity across all held stocks. Bouchaud's framework has an explicit, sourced mechanism (σ/V) predicting Apple — one of the highest-dollar-volume stocks in the world — should have a *below-average* multiplier.
2. **Exposure (how much of Apple is held in mechanically passive vehicles) and elasticity (how much a dollar of flow into or out of Apple moves its price) are different quantities, and the corpus's evidence splits cleanly along that line.** Easley et al. speaks to exposure and implies it is plausibly high for Apple (near-universal inclusion in the "Very Passive," 42%-of-AUM tail of cap-weighted trackers). Bouchaud speaks to elasticity/impact-per-dollar and implies it is plausibly *low* for Apple specifically because of its exceptional trading volume. A report that conflates these two — "Apple is heavily owned by index funds, therefore its multiple is heavily flow-supported" — would be committing exactly the error Bouchaud's mechanism warns against: high ownership does not mechanically imply high price-impact-per-marginal-dollar when the stock's own liquidity absorbs that flow more easily than a median stock's would.
3. **No paper anywhere in the corpus computes, or attempts to compute, an Apple-specific number for either quantity.** This is confirmed by a full-body keyword search of all three new sources (zero hits for "Apple," "AAPL," "mega-cap" in KRY; no Apple-specific breakdown in Easley et al.; no equity application at all in Banafti & Lee) plus a vault-wide search for Apple-specific passive-ownership or index-weight figures (Vanguard/BlackRock/State Street stakes, S&P 500 weight), which returned zero results. This is a genuine audited absence, not a gap I am filling by assumption.

### Factor crowding (brief, per instructions)

The corpus's crowding evidence for this locus is thin and explicitly flagged in contradiction-graph cluster `factor-crowding-tail-risk` (rank 19) as low decision-relevance. Two independent, non-anchoring voices: (1) MSCI — an interested party selling crowding models — reports its crowding factor has a global information ratio of −1.5 (1997–2024) with 2–3 month signal persistence ([[crowd-control-momentum-and-concentrated-markets-msci]]), and documents a real June–July 2025 quant unwind where quality/profitability underperformed in a "junk rally" even as the market rose ~8% ([[unraveling-summer-2025s-quant-fund-wobble-msci]]) — but that source explicitly states it "does not name Apple or the 'Magnificent 7'" and calls its own crowding-unwind narrative "plausible" rather than proven, citing lack of holdings data. (2) Barroso, Edelen & Karehnke (JFQA 2022, peer-reviewed but read at abstract level only) find crowding generates tail risk only when arbitrageurs ignore feedback effects, and empirically find a *negative* relation between institutional-holdings crowding proxies and expected crash risk — casting doubt on crowding as a standalone tail-risk source. The third document, "Not All Factors Crowd Equally," is a **self-withdrawn single-author preprint** whose author explicitly states the empirical sections (5–7) — which is where its momentum/reversal crash-probability asymmetry numbers and its ETF-volume-crowding correlation (ρ=−0.63) live — are "insufficient to support the global applicability claims." Per instructions, this preprint must not anchor anything; it is mentioned only to note that even its own author does not stand behind the numbers a hurried reader might otherwise cite.

Net: the corpus supports "crowding is a live, debated mechanism in the general factor-investing literature" but does not support any claim about crowding *in Apple specifically*, in either direction.

## Committed position

The literature genuinely cannot produce an Apple-specific point estimate for the passive-flow share of its multiple, and any report that presents one is manufacturing precision the demand-system literature does not contain — but the literature is not silent: two independent mechanisms (KRY's demand-system architecture, which structurally cannot generate a firm-specific multiplier because it imposes constant per-investor elasticity across all held stocks, and Bouchaud's liquidity theory, which explicitly predicts multiplier ∝ σ/volume) both point the same direction — away from Apple being a high-multiplier outlier — even though they disagree on why. Combined with Easley et al.'s finding that genuinely mechanical passive money is concentrated in large cap-weighted trackers where Apple sits near-universally at full index weight, the right two-part framing for the final report is: Apple's *exposure* to mechanical passive ownership is plausibly at the high end of the market (it is a top-2-3 constituent of nearly every major US index), but its *price-impact-per-dollar-of-flow* (the actual multiplier relevant to "how much of the multiple is flow-supported") is plausibly *below* the aggregate GK/KRY estimate precisely because its exceptional liquidity dampens impact, per the same mechanism that makes the aggregate estimate ($3–8, or 14% aggregate repricing over the 2007–2016 shift) too high to apply directly to a single mega-cap. The load-bearing reason: the aggregate estimates are calibrated to the whole market including the median (much less liquid) stock, and every mechanism in the corpus that varies by firm size or liquidity (Bouchaud's σ/V, the square-root law's Q/V_T scaling) says the multiplier shrinks, not grows, as volume rises — there is no countervailing mechanism in the corpus predicting the opposite for high-volume names.

- **Position:** No number in the literature can be safely converted into an Apple-specific passive-flow share of the multiple; the defensible range is qualitative-directional — Apple's flow/passive-artefact contribution to its multiple is more likely to sit *below* the aggregate ~14%-over-9-years (KRY) or $3–8-per-$1 (GK) benchmarks than above them, on the strength of two independent, differently-mechanized arguments (KRY's structural elasticity-homogeneity and Bouchaud's σ/V liquidity discount), while Apple's *ownership exposure* to mechanically passive vehicles is plausibly above the market median given its near-universal full-weight inclusion in cap-weighted index funds.
- **Confidence:** medium. High confidence that no Apple-specific number exists in the literature (verified by full-body search across all three new sources plus a vault-wide search). Medium confidence in the directional claim (below-aggregate multiplier), because it rests on extrapolating two theoretical mechanisms to Apple rather than on a direct Apple estimate — and Bouchaud's own multiplier (M≈1 at monthly horizon) is itself a reinterpretation, not independently re-estimated in this corpus.
- **Boundary conditions:** This directional claim applies to the *steady-state, liquid-market* multiplier relevant to ordinary rebalancing flows. It explicitly does NOT apply to tail/crisis dynamics — Bouchaud's square-root law and the σ/V relationship describe normal-regime price impact; both GK and Bouchaud note different, generally larger, effects can dominate at short horizons or in stressed/illiquid conditions (also consistent with the MSCI June–July 2025 unwind evidence showing large, fast factor-driven moves in exactly the "quality" mega-cap-adjacent space, independent of overall market direction). It also does not speak to *absolute dollar* flow-driven demand for Apple shares, which could still be very large even at a below-average per-dollar multiplier, simply because the numerator (dollars flowing in via index rebalancing, given Apple's index weight) is unusually large.
- **What would change this position:** A firm-level or size-decile-level re-estimation of the KRY demand system (or an equivalent demand-system paper) that reports elasticities or counterfactual repricing broken out by market-cap decile or by individual mega-cap identity would directly settle this — its absence from the corpus is the actual bottleneck, not a failure of search effort (three papers were read in full for this reason). A direct empirical test of Bouchaud's σ/V cross-sectional prediction using actual large-cap trading data (confirming or refuting that AAPL's realized multiplier sits below peers matched on size but with lower volume) would also be decisive. Absent either, this position should be read as the best available synthesis of two theory-level mechanisms, not an empirical finding about Apple.
- **Evidence weight:** 2 aggregate empirical estimates (GK/KRY: 14% aggregate repricing, $3–8 multiplier) neither of which is Apple-specific; 1 theoretical reinterpretation with an explicit, sourced cross-sectional mechanism pointing toward a lower Apple multiplier (Bouchaud); 1 structural/architectural finding from full-text reading that the leading demand-system paper cannot produce firm-level multipliers by construction (KRY); 1 taxonomy paper (Easley et al.) supporting high Apple ownership-exposure without addressing elasticity; 0 sources with any Apple-specific number for either quantity. On crowding: 1 interested-party vendor study, 1 abstract-level peer-reviewed paper finding crowding does NOT reliably cause tail risk, 1 self-withdrawn preprint excluded from anchoring — 0 sources naming Apple.
