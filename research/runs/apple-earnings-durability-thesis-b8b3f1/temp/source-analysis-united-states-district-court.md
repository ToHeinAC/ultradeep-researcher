## Thesis / Central claim

Having found Google liable for illegal monopoly maintenance in general search and general search text ads (Aug. 2024 liability opinion, a separate document), Judge Mehta orders a remedy package built on three pillars: (1) ban all *exclusive* distribution contracts for Search, Chrome, Assistant and Gemini and cap contract "stickiness" via mandatory annual re-election of default rather than multi-year lock-in; (2) require Google to share search-index and user-side data and offer search/ads syndication to "Qualified Competitors" for a period (generally five years within the six-year judgment) to erode Google's scale advantage; (3) explicitly decline the two most aggressive remedies plaintiffs sought — a full payment ban and Chrome/Android divestiture — because the court found both would cause **more** harm to consumers and distributors than they would fix, at least "for now." The judgment runs six years, effective 60 days after entry (Sept. 2 2025 + 60 days ≈ Nov. 2025), except Technical Committee provisions, effective immediately.

## Methodology / Basis of claims

This is not an empirical study; it is a judicial opinion applying Sherman Act §2 remedy doctrine (Microsoft III's "fit the wrong," causal-connection, and non-punitive-but-market-restoring standards) to an evidentiary record built during a three-week 2025 remedies trial (~50 witnesses, hundreds of exhibits) plus the carried-forward Aug. 2024 liability findings. Load-bearing evidentiary inputs the court weighs: (a) live/deposition testimony from Apple SVP Eddy Cue, Google CEO Sundar Pichai, Google's Android chief Sameer Samat, economists Dr. Tasneem Chipty (plaintiffs) and Dr. Kevin Murphy (Google), and executives from Mozilla, Motorola, Samsung, T-Mobile, Verizon, Perplexity, OpenAI-adjacent witnesses; (b) the "Allcott Study," an academic economic model of a Bing-default counterfactual; (c) the court's own prior liability findings (747 F. Supp. 3d 1) which it treats as established fact, not re-litigated. The court repeatedly signals it is exercising equitable discretion under uncertainty ("gaze into a crystal ball… [n]ot exactly a judge's forte," p.2) and explicitly declines to require "mathematical precision" in causal attribution (p.98).

## Key findings / Claims (with specific numbers where present)

1. **Payment ban rejected — payments for default placement are explicitly permitted, with no price or magnitude condition attached.** "So, for now, Google will be permitted to pay distributors for default placement." (p.128, RCOL III.A). This is unconditional as to amount — the court does not cap payment size, only contract structure (no exclusivity, annual reset). VERIFIED against the fetcher's headline claim.

2. **The court's central reasoning for rejecting the ban has two independent legs, both explicit:** (a) harm to distributors/consumers — "[c]utting off payments from Google almost certainly will impose substantial—in some cases, crippling—downstream harms to distribution partners, related markets, and consumers" (top-line summary, p.4); a payment ban would hand Google a "Hobson's choice" outcome where, on "day one," distributors keep Google anyway (best product) but stop getting paid — "Google thus would continue to receive a disproportionate volume of search queries for a fraction of the cost. Freed of having to pay billions in revenue share, Google's profits would increase. **Not paying Apple alone would result in a windfall worth tens of billions of dollars**" (p.121-122, citing 2022 Apple revenue share estimate of **~$20 billion**, Google 747 F. Supp. 3d at 90 ¶299 — note: explicitly a *worldwide* figure, and the court flags that Apple's U.S.-only figure would be lower but still "billions"). (b) consumer-welfare risk — both parties' economists agreed a ban would likely produce *lower* payments to distributors in the near term even in the scenario where rivals win share, because "with Google sidelined from competition, rivals would pay less than Google did to secure default or preferential placement" (p.122-123) — this is a critical, symmetric finding: the court's own logic implies degraded distributor payments under *any* scenario that removes Google's willingness to overpay for defaults, whether via ban or (implicitly) via loss of exclusivity leverage.

3. **The court explicitly rejects plaintiffs' expert's 31% share-shift estimate as "too speculative."** Full quote, footnote 11 (p.120): "Although not essential to its conclusion, the court did not find Dr. Chipty's share-shifting exercise to be persuasive. The potential 31% market shift of queries to competitors struck the court as too speculative and not grounded in realistic assumptions about market behavior." The court notes Chipty's own assumptions shifted under cross ("she later acknowledged that such a switch might not occur on 'day one'"). VERIFIED. This finding is directly relevant to durability: if the court doubts defaults will actually move much traffic even under aggressive remedies, this cuts against a large probability-weighted downside to Apple's TAC from *demand-side* erosion (users abandoning Google) — but it says nothing about *price* erosion (Google paying less because it no longer needs exclusivity to keep the default).

4. **2021 industry-wide TAC figure confirmed: "In 2021, Google paid more than $26 billion in 'traffic acquisition costs' to distribution partners."** (p.119, citing Google, 747 F. Supp. 3d at 88-89 ¶289). The court itself adds: "That number has likely grown since." VERIFIED, exact figure and date.

5. **Exclusivity ban — confirmed for Search, Chrome, Assistant, and Gemini, with an enumerated four-part injunction.** Top-line text (p.3-4): Google "shall not enter or maintain any agreement that (1) conditions the licensing of the Play Store or any other Google application on the distribution, preloading, or placement of Google Search, Chrome, Google Assistant, or the Gemini app anywhere on a device; (2) conditions the receipt of revenue share payments for the placement of one Google application… on the placement of another such application; (3) conditions the receipt of revenue share payments on maintaining Google Search, Chrome, Google Assistant, or the Gemini app on any device, browser, or search access point for **more than one year**; or (4) prohibits any partner from simultaneously distributing any other GSE, browser, or GenAI product." VERIFIED.

6. **Annual re-election / one-year term confirmed and extended by the court beyond what either party proposed for browser deals.** Google's own proposal allowed Browser Developers (i.e., Apple) to "annually set a different GSE at various search access points across different devices" as a condition of receiving revenue share (p.109-110, Google's RPFJ §III.K-L) — the court adopted this AND extended the same one-year condition to OEM/carrier agreements, which Google's draft had NOT included: "the court will impose the same one-year option as a condition of payments to OEMs and wireless carriers" (p.110-111). The court's stated rationale: "[r]ivals cannot presently access [default] channels of distribution without convincing Google's partners to break existing agreements, all of which are binding for a term of years" — i.e., contract *duration* itself (not just exclusivity) was found anticompetitive. VERIFIED — this is the annual-rebidding architecture the fetcher flagged.

7. **Six-year term confirmed, but decomposed explicitly by the court as "five years, plus an additional one year" for stand-up/administrative purposes** — not a single undifferentiated six-year clock. "[T]he court believes that a five-year term, plus an additional one year to put the infrastructure and processes in place to implement the remedies, is appropriate." (p.220). Effective date: "the final judgment shall take effect 60 days after it is entered, except as to those portions of Section X.A of the Plaintiffs' RPFJ that require the parties to take steps toward forming the Technical Committee… which will be effective immediately." (p.220). VERIFIED. Google had sought 3 years/120-day delay; plaintiffs sought 10 years/30-day delay. The court explicitly invoked the Microsoft consent decree's five-year term and its own rationale ("pace of technological change") as precedent, and separately said a 10-year term "runs the risk of growing stale" given GenAI's pace (p.220-221) — i.e., the court's own stated reason for a *shorter-than-plaintiffs-wanted* term is that AI competition, not the decree itself, is what the court is betting on to discipline Google.

8. **Apple-specific GenAI modification confirmed, narrowly targeted at Safari and Apple devices.** "[T]o leave no doubt that Apple falls within the revision, Section III.L of Google's RPFJ shall be modified to make clear that Google is prohibited from entering into an exclusive agreement with Apple to distribute any Google GenAI product either in any Safari mode or on any Apple mobile or desktop device." (p.115). This closes a gap in Google's own draft, which defined "Third Party Generative AI Assistive Service" too narrowly (limited to "stand-alone mobile" chatbots) to clearly cover a possible future exclusive Gemini-Safari tie-up. VERIFIED.

9. **Chrome divestiture and contingent Android divestiture both rejected outright** — no five-year trigger review, no revisit mechanism specific to structural relief (contrast with the explicit "prepared to revisit a payment ban" language, finding #14 below). Reasoning: causal-connection standard for structural remedies not met (Google's dominance not shown to be primarily attributable to the illegal conduct vs. lawful factors — quality, R&D, brand); Chrome is not geographically confined to the U.S. (>80% of MAUs outside U.S.); technical/operational entanglement with Google's back-end infrastructure makes a clean sale implausible without "substantial product degradation." Plaintiffs' own expert (Chipty) estimated only a **7% share shift** from Chrome divestiture even if fully executed (p.112-113) — modest relative to the rejected 31% payment-ban estimate.

10. **Choice screens rejected, with an unusually candid admission that a remedy fashioned around device-level "choice" is pointless absent product-quality parity.** Apple's Cue, called by Google, testified: "[Apple] would 'do a choice screen, but it's not going to matter. It's not going to matter until one of the choices is actually really valuable that provides new capabilities.'" (p.191, Rem. Tr. 3849:4-7). Corroborated by the "Allcott Study" academic model: European choice screens moved Google's desktop share by only 1.3 percentage points and consumer surplus by "$0.07" — "Choice screens increase consumer surplus, but they barely move the needle in terms of market shares." (p.191).

11. **Google's own "prophylactic" contract changes are already in effect and treated by the court as a fact on the ground, not a promise.** Google updated its RSAs pre-trial to remove exclusivity-adjacent provisions and sent "eve-of-trial" waiver letters to three Android partners loosening restrictions on preloading rival "Alternative Assistive Products" (p.106-107, FOF ¶¶94-103). The court treats this as evidence the market has *already* partially moved before the final judgment forces it to — a fact relevant to whether the remedy is "toothless" (see §6 below) since some of what the injunction requires, Google had already conceded.

12. **Data-sharing and syndication remedies are time-bounded, not permanent, and are explicitly a bridge, not an end-state.** "Qualified Competitors will be able to deliver high-quality web results **for five years** while they build their own search index and search stack." (p.179-180). Search Text Ads syndication license term for Qualified Competitors set at market terms ("financial terms no worse than" Google's own AdSense-for-Search customers), with Qualified Competitors free to set minimum CPCs (p.184-185). These remedies target Google's *rivals'* scale deficit, not Apple's distribution rent directly, but they matter to the durability thesis because they are the mechanism by which the court hopes a genuine rival search product could emerge within the judgment's own six-year window — which would then be the actual driver of TAC repricing, not the injunction's contract-form rules per se.

13. **GenAI usage/market-share figures as of trial (spring 2025), useful magnitude anchors for the "does the rent migrate to AI assistants" question:** OpenAI's own Dec. 2024 estimate of U.S. GenAI-assistant market share: ChatGPT ~85%, Gemini ~7%, Claude ~3%, Perplexity/Copilot the remainder (p.45, FOF ¶60). Google's internal estimate of daily queries as of March 28, 2025: Gemini ~140 million, **ChatGPT ~1.2 billion**, Meta AI >200 million, Grok ~75 million, DeepSeek ~50 million, Perplexity ~30 million (p.46, FOF ¶60). ChatGPT had "over 100 million daily active users as of the end of 2024" (p.42, FOF ¶52). Separately: "Search queries in Apple's Safari web browser declined for the first time in 22 years" — Apple's own witness Eddy Cue attributed this to GenAI chatbot emergence (p.100-101, FOF, Rem. Tr. 3818:20-3819:4, 3846:24-3847:3). VERIFIED as a court finding, not mere commentary — this is Apple's own SVP testifying under oath, called as **Google's** witness, that Safari Google-search volume is already eroding.

14. **Apple pays OpenAI (not the reverse) for the ChatGPT-in-Apple-Intelligence integration.** "In December 2024, Apple began integrating ChatGPT and OpenAI technology into its devices to power Apple Intelligence… in exchange for revenue share payments." (p.42, FOF ¶52, citing Rem. Tr. 3832:4-3833:11, 3838:17-3839:18 (Cue)). The direction of payment is not made fully explicit in this excerpt but the framing ("Apple began integrating… in exchange for revenue share payments") together with industry-standard structure suggests Apple is paying OpenAI, or minimally sharing subscription-conversion revenue — this is the opposite direction of the Apple-Google TAC flow and should not be conflated with it. Flagged as a point the depth investigator should verify against Apple's own disclosures, since the opinion's wording is not 100% unambiguous on payment direction.

15. **The court explicitly reserves the right to revisit the payment ban if the remedy underperforms** — a live, undated trigger mechanism, not a scheduled review: "The court is thus prepared to revisit a payment ban (or a lesser remedy) if competition is not substantially restored through the remedies the court does impose." (p.128). Footnote 14 (p.128) further floats a middle-ground remedy the court did NOT adopt but flagged as live for the future: "allowing Google to make payments for distribution but not for **default** distribution, also referred to as 'unconditional revenue share'" — the court says it lacked a sufficient record on this option now, implying it is a plausible remedy on a future record. This is a named, specific "remedy design" the depth investigator/critics should treat as a real tail scenario, not a straw man.

16. **The court's own account of why it chose 5+1 years over plaintiffs' 10: it is betting on GenAI competition, not the decree, to do the disciplining.** Direct quote (p.220-221) citing Apple's Cue: "'[F]or the first time, I think there's an opportunity [to challenge Google] because there's new technology…' … Technological advancement and product differentiation are what will change market dynamics, not a decade-long judicial decree." This is the single clearest piece of text in the opinion linking the court's remedy design to a specific view about AI-driven disruption timing — i.e., the court's own theory of the case is that if TAC value is going to erode, it is GenAI competition that does it, not the injunction's contract rules.

## Load-bearing citations / sources this source depends on

- **Google, 747 F. Supp. 3d 1 (D.D.C. 2024)** — the Aug. 5, 2024 liability opinion (a separate document being analyzed in parallel by another source-analyst per the pipeline note). This remedies opinion treats essentially all liability-phase factual findings as established and cites them extensively (e.g., the $20B 2022 Apple TAC estimate, the "frozen" ecosystem finding, the 80%→89.2% market share growth 2009-2020, the 50%/20%/90% query-flow breakdowns). Any depth investigation of TAC magnitude should pull the underlying ¶289 finding directly from that document.
- **Microsoft III, 253 F.3d 34 (D.C. Cir. 2001) (en banc)** — the controlling appellate framework for antitrust remedy design ("fit the wrong," causal-connection standards for structural vs. behavioral remedies, "clearer indication of a significant causal connection" test). This is the doctrinal backbone of nearly every "why we didn't go further" passage in the opinion.
- **New York I / Massachusetts, 224 F. Supp. 2d 76 (D.D.C. 2002); 373 F.3d 1199 (D.C. Cir. 2004)** — the Microsoft consent-decree remedies opinions, cited as direct precedent for the Technical Committee mechanism, the five-year term choice, and for extending remedies to technologies (there: middleware/servers; here: GenAI) not squarely part of the liability-phase market definition.
- **The "Allcott Study"** (cited as "Allcott Study at 34-36" etc.) — an academic economic model (Bing-as-default counterfactual and choice-screen counterfactual) that the court leans on for two separate empirical conclusions: (a) a payment ban would likely make Bing the default and cause "a large reduction in consumer surplus"; (b) choice screens barely move market share (1.3pp) or consumer surplus ($0.07). This is a **named non-replicated dataset/model** the depth investigator should try to locate and verify independently — it is doing real analytical work in the opinion's rejection of two of plaintiffs' remedies, and its assumptions (especially the "permanently inattentive users" framing) are worth scrutinizing.
- **Dr. Tasneem Chipty's expert testimony/exhibits (PXRD012, RDXD-33.023, etc.)** — plaintiffs' economist; her share-shift estimates (31% for payment ban, 7% for Chrome divestiture) are both discussed and the former explicitly rejected as unreliable. Any citation of "31% share shift" downstream MUST carry the court's rejection alongside it — it is Chipty's estimate, described by the court as unpersuasive, not the court's own finding.
- **Dr. Kevin Murphy's testimony** (Google's economist) — corroborates, from the opposite side, that payments would likely be lower in a non-Google-anchored world ("I would expect to see lower payments to partners" with "the highest valued customer for your promotion… off the table," p.123). Notably the two opposing experts *agree* on this point even though they disagreed on almost everything else — this convergence is analytically load-bearing for the durability thesis.
- **In re Google Play Store Antitrust Litig., 2025 WL 2167402 (9th Cir. 2025)** — recent Ninth Circuit affirmance of network-effects-targeted remedies (Play Store catalog sharing), cited repeatedly to justify going beyond mere behavioral injunction into affirmative data-sharing/syndication requirements, and to reject Google's Trinko-based "forced sharing" objection.
- **Herbert Hovenkamp, "Antitrust and Platform Monopoly," 130 Yale L.J. 1952 (2021)** and **Areeda & Hovenkamp, Antitrust Law treatise** — academic authority the court cites both for and against a payment ban (Hovenkamp is quoted suggesting a payment ban "could... be enjoined," yet the court declines to follow that suggestion), and for the "network monopoly... may have to be forced open by more aggressive means" principle used to justify data-sharing.

## Caveats, limitations, contradictions

- **This opinion predates its own appellate history.** Filed Sept. 2, 2025; today's date (per this run) is Sept. 13, 2026. The document is necessarily silent on whether either side appealed, on what grounds, or on any stay pending appeal. Nothing in the text should be read as addressing appellate timing or probability — this is an audited absence, not an oversight: THE OPINION ITSELF CANNOT SPEAK TO ITS OWN APPEAL. The depth investigator must source appeal status separately (e.g., D.C. Circuit docket, subsequent Apple 10-K/10-Q risk-factor language, news coverage after Sept 2025).
- **The 60-day effective date is measured from entry of the *final judgment*, not from this Sept. 2, 2025 memorandum opinion.** The court ordered the parties to submit a "revised final judgment" by Sept. 10, 2025, consistent with this opinion (p.222) — meaning the operative injunction document is a later, separate filing this analyst has not reviewed. The precise effective date, and any interim modifications negotiated between the parties in that joint-status process, are NOT captured by this document and should be verified against the actual entered final judgment (a different docket entry, later than Doc. 1436).
- **The $20 billion 2022 Apple-Google figure is explicitly flagged by the court itself as a worldwide number,** while the underlying payment-ban proposal and much of the "windfall" reasoning conflate worldwide and domestic scope. The court acknowledges this directly in footnote 12 (p.123): "the court recognizes that the Apple payment figure is a worldwide number... In any event, Google undoubtedly pays Apple billions of dollars for domestic distribution." No precise U.S.-only figure appears anywhere in this opinion.
- **The court repeatedly says it "cannot predict to any degree of certainty"** whether the harms it hypothesizes from a payment ban would occur (p.125) — its refusal to ban payments is explicitly a risk-management judgment under uncertainty, not a finding that payments will definitely continue at current levels. This matters for treating TAC as a "repriceable annuity": the court's own language supports continuation of *some* payment, not a specific magnitude or trajectory.
- **Internal tension: the court simultaneously (a) doubts a 31% share-shift will occur (implying defaults still matter enormously and Google will keep paying to protect them) and (b) credits both economists' shared view that non-exclusive competitive bidding would likely produce *lower* payments even without any share shift at all** (because rivals, absent Google in the running, would bid less than Google historically overpaid). These are not strictly contradictory (traffic volume ≠ price per unit of traffic) but the opinion does not itself reconcile them into a single quantitative view of *net* TAC trajectory — that synthesis is left to the reader/downstream analyst.
- **The opinion's own framing of the six-year term as a bet on GenAI competition, not the decree, cuts against reading the decree itself as the primary mechanism disciplining Google's willingness to pay.** If the court's own theory is that AI-driven product differentiation (not contract-form rules) is what will erode Google's position, then a "the annual-reset clause forces price competition" story is at most a secondary mechanism in the court's own stated worldview — the primary mechanism the court is counting on is exogenous technological change.
- **No self-preferencing, anti-retaliation, or anti-circumvention provisions were adopted** — the court found these too vague under Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(d) and evidentially unsupported (pp.206-213). This means the injunction has fewer teeth against sophisticated future workarounds than plaintiffs sought; a critic could read this as supporting the "toothless" reading (see below).

## Relevance to research_query

This document is the single most important primary source for research question 2 and directly informs the Phase 3/Phase 4 durability classification the query specifies. It establishes, in the court's own words rather than commentary, that (a) Google TAC payments to Apple are not banned and face no price ceiling — the "binary" event of a payment ban did NOT occur, so that specific tail risk has already resolved to zero for at least the life of this judgment (6 years, i.e., spanning the entire 2-5 year holding horizon in view); (b) what the court DID impose — the exclusivity ban plus mandatory annual re-election — changes the bargaining structure of the Apple-Google relationship from a multi-year exclusive lock-in to an annually recontestable non-exclusive arrangement, which the court's own economic evidence (both parties' experts agreeing) suggests should compress the *price* Google is willing to pay, even though the court doubts much *volume* will actually shift; (c) the judgment's six-year term (5+1, effective 60 days post-entry of the not-yet-reviewed final judgment) covers the entire query horizon, meaning "wait for appellate reversal" is not something a 2-5 year investor can count on resolving mid-horizon with confidence, since the opinion is silent on appeal timing; (d) the Apple-specific GenAI modification closes off one specific tail scenario (an exclusive Gemini-in-Safari deal) without saying anything about a *non-exclusive* revenue-sharing GenAI arrangement, which remains open under the same annual-reset logic as search. This source directly satisfies the query's instruction to extract "REMEDY DESIGN" analysis even where the ultimate outcome (appeal) is not predictable — the remedy itself, its reasoning, and its explicit self-reserved revisit trigger are all fully analysable from this text alone.

## The "toothless remedy" versus "structural erosion" dispute — textual evidence for each reading

**Evidence supporting "toothless"/status-quo reading:**
- Plaintiffs' own framing, adopted nowhere disputed by the court as mischaracterization: Google's preferred remedy (prohibitory injunction only) would "merely 'maintain the status quo' and do nothing to restore competition" (p.16) — and the court's actual remedy is Google's proposal *plus* modifications, not a wholesale departure from it.
- Payment ban, Chrome divestiture, Android divestiture, choice screens, self-preferencing bar, anti-retaliation/anti-circumvention provisions, granular ad-data sharing, exact-match keyword restoration, public education fund, and publisher remedies were ALL rejected — nearly every remedy with the most direct, immediate financial bite on Google was declined.
- The court's own words: Google "still is likely to select Google as its primary, if not only, default GSE" even under the new rules, "due in large part to... network effects" (p.108-109) — the court itself predicts limited near-term behavioral change from distributors.
- Apple's own Cue on choice screens: "it's not going to matter" absent a genuinely better alternative (p.191) — direct testimony that procedural remedies without product quality parity change nothing.
- Google had already implemented much of what the injunction requires via "prophylactic" contract amendments before the ruling (p.106-107) — suggesting the marginal bite of the order beyond what Google conceded voluntarily may be modest.

**Evidence supporting "structural erosion"/meaningful-change reading:**
- Contract *duration* itself, not just exclusivity, is now capped at one year for ALL distribution channels (browsers, OEMs, carriers) — a genuinely new constraint Google's own proposal did not include for OEMs/carriers, extended by the court sua sponte (p.110-111). Annual recontestability is a structural change to bargaining leverage even if near-term volume doesn't shift.
- Both sides' economists agree non-exclusive, Google-optional distribution would likely produce *lower* payments even without volume loss — a quantifiable, if unquantified-in-dollars, prediction of price erosion baked into the court's own reasoning (pp.122-123).
- Five-year data-sharing and syndication remedies are explicitly designed to let "Qualified Competitors... build their own search index and search stack" (p.179-180) — a scale-based structural intervention aimed at removing the underlying reason (data scale) Google can outbid everyone, not merely a contract-form fix.
- The court reserves an explicit, undated trigger to revisit the payment ban "if competition is not substantially restored" (p.128) — a live regulatory sword, not a closed question, that persists for the life of the six-year judgment and could bind mid-holding-period.
- The court's own chosen term (5+1 years, not Google's 3) was lengthened specifically because it judged 3 years insufficient for "positive market impacts" to emerge (p.220) — i.e., the court itself believes real change is more likely on a 5-6 year horizon than a 3-year one, which is squarely inside the query's 2-5 year window.

**This analyst's reading, from the text alone:** the opinion is neither purely cosmetic nor a decisive structural break. It removes one specific, quantifiable tail risk (payment ban / Chrome-Android divestiture) essentially to zero for the judgment's six-year life, while leaving in place a plausible, evidence-supported (both experts agree) mechanism for gradual price compression via loss of exclusivity leverage and annual recontestability — without the court itself being willing to size that compression. The correct read for a TAC durability classification is: NOT a binary all-or-nothing event class (the biggest binary risk already resolved favorably for continued payments), but also NOT a static repriceable annuity at the current ~$20B/worldwide, ~mid-teens-billions-USD run rate — rather a **repriceable annuity with a court-imposed downward-biased repricing mechanism** (annual reset removes Google's ability to lock in a premium for exclusivity) whose magnitude the opinion deliberately does not quantify, compounded by a genuinely separate, larger, and unquantified GenAI-migration risk that the court's own reasoning (not this analyst's inference) treats as the dominant threat to Google's position over the judgment's term.

## Scenario values for Apple TAC, 2-5 year horizon — what the text supports and where it is silent

1. **Status quo continues under annual terms.** TEXT SUPPORTS AS PLAUSIBLE: the court expects distributors, including implicitly Apple, to keep Google as default near-term ("we have to pick what's best for our customers, and today, that is still Google" — Cue, p.109) and imposes no payment cap. TEXT IS SILENT on: whether Apple exercises its new annual right to solicit competing bids, and what magnitude of discount (if any) Google would need to offer to retain the default once bidding is contestable rather than exclusive.

2. **Payments decline gradually under non-exclusivity / annual re-bidding.** TEXT SUPPORTS AS THE COURT'S OWN ECONOMIC EXPECTATION (from both parties' experts): "it is quite possible that in the remedial world payments will be lower. And in the initial years" (Chipty, p.123); Murphy: "I would expect to see lower payments to partners" (p.123). TEXT IS SILENT on magnitude, timing (beyond "initial years"), or whether decline is a one-time repricing at each annual renewal versus a continuous erosion.

3. **Appellate reversal toward a stricter remedy (payment ban imposed).** TEXT IS ENTIRELY SILENT — opinion predates any appeal; the court's own reasoning for rejecting the ban (harm to distributors/consumers, insufficiently certain benefit, "caution is key" per Alston) would need to be overturned or superseded, which is a legal/appellate question outside this document's scope. What the text DOES support: if this scenario materializes, the court's own analysis implies the *domestic* Apple windfall-to-Google-avoided (i.e., Apple's lost revenue) would be "billions of dollars" but below the cited $20B worldwide 2022 figure (p.123, footnote 12) — a ceiling-side data point for sizing this scenario, not a probability.

4. **Appellate reversal toward Google on liability (remedy vacated entirely).** TEXT IS ENTIRELY SILENT — this document assumes liability as settled and does not contemplate its own reversal. Not addressable from this source.

5. **A middle-ground remedy (payments allowed, but not for *default* placement — "unconditional revenue share") is adopted on a future record.** TEXT EXPLICITLY FLAGS THIS AS LIVE BUT UNADOPTED: "the court is presently ill-equipped to consider it" for lack of a developed record (footnote 14, p.128) — meaning this is a named, judicially-contemplated remedy that could be revisited within the six-year term if plaintiffs build a better record, but it is NOT currently in effect and has no timetable.

## Implications for the durability classification

**TAC should be classified as a repriceable annuity subject to a court-imposed, one-directional (downward-biased) repricing mechanism — NOT as a binary event.** The single genuinely binary event this record contains (payment ban / structural divestiture) has already resolved in the favorable direction for continued payments, and this resolution is locked in for the six-year life of the judgment (i.e., spans the entire 2-5 year holding horizon), subject only to two residual binary tails the text itself acknowledges but does not quantify or date: (a) the court's self-reserved right to revisit the ban if competition fails to develop (undated, evidence-triggered, not currently pending); (b) appellate proceedings, about which this document — filed before any appeal could have been noticed — is necessarily and completely silent. Within the "keeps paying" branch, which the text treats as the operative near-term reality, the analytically correct move is NOT to treat the payment stream as a flat annuity either: the court itself, via both parties' economists in agreement, expects the switch from exclusive multi-year contracts to non-exclusive annually-recontestable ones to compress the price Google is willing to pay, with magnitude and speed left unquantified in this opinion. A defensible 2-5 year modeling posture consistent with this text: base case = gradual, single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage annual erosion in Google's TAC payment to Apple as annual renewals pass, driven by loss of exclusivity leverage rather than volume loss (since the court doubts material volume/traffic will shift); separately and additively, an unquantified but text-substantiated GenAI-migration risk that the court's own reasoning identifies as the more consequential threat to the entire arrangement's economic logic over a multi-year horizon — Apple's own witness testifying, under oath, that Safari's Google-search volume already declined for the first time in 22 years, attributed to GenAI chatbot emergence, is the load-bearing fact here, not the injunction's contract-form provisions.
